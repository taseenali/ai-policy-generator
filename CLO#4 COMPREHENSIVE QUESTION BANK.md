## CHAPTER 19 — WATER POLLUTION AND TREATMENT

### SUBJECTIVE / ESSAY

**Q19.1** [S19 Q1] Do you think outbreaks of waterborne diseases will be more common or less common in the future? Why? Where are outbreaks most likely to occur?

**Answer**: More common. Human populations continue to grow, increasing risk. Primary water-pollution problem globally is lack of clean drinking water. Each year several billion people in less-developed countries are exposed to waterborne diseases (effects range from upset stomach to death). Outbreaks continue to be a threat even in developed countries.

---

**Q19.2** [S19 Q2] What was learned from the Exxon Valdez oil spill that might help reduce the number of future spills and their environmental impact?

**Answer**: The Exxon Valdez spill produced an environmental shock that resulted in passage of the Oil Pollution Act of 1990 and a renewed evaluation of cleanup technology.

---

**Q19.3** [S19 Q3] What is meant by the term water pollution, and what are several major processes that contribute to water pollution?

**Answer**: Water pollution refers to degradation of water quality. In defining pollution, we generally look at the intended use of the water, how far the water departs from the norm, its effects on public health, or its ecological impacts. Water pollutants include heavy metals, sediment, certain radioactive isotopes, heat, fecal coliform bacteria, phosphorus, nitrogen, sodium, and other useful (even necessary) elements, as well as certain pathogenic bacteria and viruses. All segments of society—urban, rural, industrial, agricultural, and military—may contribute to the problem of water pollution.

---

**Q19.4** [S19 Q4] Compare and contrast point and nonpoint sources of water pollution. Which is easier to treat, and why?

**Answer**: Point sources are distinct and confined sites that empty into streams or rivers. Nonpoint sources, such as runoff, are diffused and intermittent and are influenced by factors such as land use, climate, hydrology, topography, native vegetation, and geology. Point source pollutants from industries are controlled through on-site treatment or disposal and are regulated by permit. Nonpoint sources are difficult to monitor and control. Point sources are easier to treat.

---

**Q19.5** [S19 Q5] What is the twofold effect of sediment pollution?

**Answer**: Sediment pollution is a twofold problem: (1) It results from erosion, which depletes a land resource (soil) at its site of origin; (2) It reduces the quality of the water resource it enters.

---

**Q19.6** [S19 Q6] In the summer, you buy a house with a septic system that appears to function properly. In the winter, effluent discharges at the surface. What could be the environmental cause of the problem? How could the problem be alleviated?

**Answer**: Most common causes are failure to pump out the septic tank when it is full of solids, and poor soil drainage, which allows the effluent to rise to the surface in wet weather. Solutions include siting septic tanks on well-drained soils, making sure systems are large enough, and practicing proper maintenance.

---

**Q19.7** [S19 Q7] Describe the major steps in wastewater treatment (primary, secondary, advanced). Can natural ecosystems perform any of these functions? Which ones?

**Answer**: Primary treatment: sewage passes through screens to remove large floating organic material, enters grit chamber where sand/stones/grit are removed, goes to primary sedimentation tank where particulate matter settles out to form sludge. Secondary treatment: uses activated sludge (living organisms—mostly bacteria). Wastewater from primary sedimentation tank enters aeration tank mixed with air and sludge containing aerobic bacteria that consume organic material (BOD). Wastewater then enters final sedimentation tank where sludge settles out. Advanced treatments: specifically designed treatments such as sand filters, carbon filters, and chemicals to remove specific pollutants. At a slower rate natural ecosystems could digest the sludge and consume organic material.

---

**Q19.8** [S19 Q8] How does water that drains from coal mines become contaminated with sulfuric acid? Why is this an important environmental problem?

**Answer**: Coal and rocks containing coal are often associated with pyrite (FeS₂), which is iron sulfide. When pyrite comes into contact with oxygen and water, it weathers. A product of the chemical weathering is sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). The acid is produced when surface water or shallow groundwater runs through or moves into and out of mines or tailings. If the acidic water runs off to a natural stream, pond, or lake, significant pollution and ecological damage may result. The acidic water is toxic to plants and animals of an aquatic ecosystem; it damages biological productivity, and fish and other aquatic life may die. Acidic water can also seep into and pollute groundwater.

---

**Q19.9** [S19 Q9] What is eutrophication, and why is it an ecosystem effect?

**Answer**: Eutrophication is the process by which a body of water develops a high concentration of nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus. The nutrients increase the growth of aquatic plants in general, as well as production of photosynthetic blue-green bacteria and algae. The bacteria and algae die, and as they decompose, BOD increases, reducing the water's oxygen content, sometimes to the point where other organisms, such as fish, will die. They die not from phosphorus poisoning but from a chain of events that started with the input of phosphorus and affected the whole ecosystem. This is what we call an ecosystem effect.

## CHAPTER 20 — ATMOSPHERE AND CLIMATE CHANGE

### SUBJECTIVE / ESSAY

**Q20.1** [AC Q1 / CW Q13] Methane in the atmosphere has more than doubled in the past 200 years. List the sources of atmospheric methane.

**Answer**:
1. Bacteria in guts of termites and intestines of ruminant mammals (cows) digesting woody plants
2. Seepage from oil fields and methane hydrates
3. Landfills (major US source)
4. Burning of biofuels
5. Production of coal and natural gas
6. Agriculture (raising cattle, cultivating rice)
7. Oxygenless freshwater wetlands (bacteria decompose vegetation, releasing methane as decay product)

---

**Q20.2** [AC Q2 / CW Q14] What is the ocean conveyor belt and what role does it play in Europe's climate?

**Answer**: The ocean conveyor belt is a global circulation of ocean waters characterized by strong northward movement of upper warm waters of the Gulf Stream in the Atlantic Ocean. The temperature of these waters is approximately 12°–13°C when they arrive near Greenland, and they are cooled in the North Atlantic to 2°–4°C. As the water cools, it becomes saltier and denser, causing it to sink to the bottom. The cold, deep current flows southward, then eastward, and finally northward in the Pacific Ocean. Upwelling in the North Pacific starts the warm, shallow current again. The flow rate is huge—20 million m³/sec, about equal to 100 Amazon Rivers. The heat released to the atmosphere from the warm water keeps Northern Europe 5°C–10°C warmer than if the ocean conveyor belt were not present.

---

**Q20.3** [AC Q3 / CW Q15] List the four levels of the atmosphere, in order, from Earth to space.

**Answer**: Troposphere, Stratosphere, Mesosphere, Thermosphere

---

**Q20.4** [AC Q4 / CW Q16] What are radionuclides and how are they used to gain information on solar cycles?

**Answer**: Radionuclides are atoms with unstable nuclei that undergo radioactive decay. They are often trapped in glacial ice and can be measured to gain information on variation in the sun's intensity during periods in history. Radioactive ¹⁴C is produced in the upper atmosphere by collision of cosmic rays and ¹⁴N. The record of ¹⁴C in the atmosphere has been correlated with tree ring chronology.

---

**Q20.5** [AC Q5 / CW Q17] Explain the difference between the terms weather and climate.

**Answer**: Weather is what's happening now or over some short time period (hour, day, week) in the atmosphere near the ground. It encompasses temperature, pressure, cloudiness, precipitation, and winds. Climate is the average weather and usually refers to average weather conditions over long periods (seasons, years, decades).

---

**Q20.6** [AC Q6 / CW Q18] Name the gases that have the greatest impact on the greenhouse effect.

**Answer**: CO₂, methane (CH₄), water vapor (H₂O), CFCs, nitrous oxide (N₂O)

---

**Q20.7** [LQ Q1] How would you characterize the changes in global average temperature since 1960? In global carbon dioxide concentrations? How do scientists relate changes in global average temperature and carbon dioxide concentrations?

**Answer**: Global average temperature has increased dramatically since 1960, by about 1°C. As carbon dioxide levels increase, there is a corresponding increase in temperature, indicating the two are intrinsically linked.

---

**Q20.8** [LQ Q2] How do greenhouse gases affect global climate conditions?

**Answer**: Because greenhouse gases absorb infrared radiation, higher greenhouse gas concentrations lead to warming and global climate change. This occurs because the absorption of heat slows its eventual reradiation into space, thereby warming the lower atmosphere. With these gases absorbed in the atmosphere, they are causing a canopy over the earth trapping in the sun's rays. This is heating up the earth's surface which in turn heats up the oceans. Ocean temperatures are warming which is causing water organisms to adapt or die. With heated oceans, and heating the earth, ice from the glaciers is melting raising sea levels.

---

**Q20.9** [LQ Q3] What are climate models? What inputs are used when building them? What can they tell us about future climates?

**Answer**: Climate models are systems of mathematical equations used to simulate the atmosphere of the earth. Climate models are used for weather forecasting, understanding the climate, and projecting climate change. Climate models take into account incoming energy from the sun as well as outgoing energy from the earth. Climate models are used to project what the climate will be like in a few decades or a century from now.

---

**Q20.10** [LQ Q4] What two factors contribute to sea-level rise?

**Answer**: (1) The thawing and melting of mountain glaciers and polar ice caps and sheets due to atmospheric warming. (2) Thermal expansion of ocean water as it heats up.

---

**Q20.11** [LQ Q5] What weather changes do climatologists expect as the global ocean warms?

**Answer**: Two major effects on weather: (1) Warming ocean water will affect the global ocean conveyor belt, reducing upwelling of cold, nutrient water and reducing distribution of heat and precipitation from equator towards poles. If the ocean conveyor belt stopped working normally, it could cause major cooling in Europe coupled with extreme heat in other areas. (2) A secondary effect is that passing over warm water increases the severity of extreme weather events such as typhoons and hurricanes.

---

**Q20.12** [LQ Q6] Austrian biologists studying plants growing high in the Alps found that plants adapted to cold-mountain conditions migrated up the peaks as fast as 3.7 m every decade during the 20th century, apparently in response to climate warming. Assuming that warming continues during the 21st century, what will happen to the plants if they reach the tops of the mountains?

**Answer**: As warming accelerates, many species will undoubtedly become extinct, particularly those with narrow temperature requirements, those confined to small, specialized habitats, and those living in fragile ecosystems. Ecosystems considered at greatest risk of loss of species in the short term are polar seas, coral reefs, mountain ecosystems, coastal wetlands, and tundra.

---

**Q20.13** [LQ Q8] What aspects of adaptation to global climate change would be easier for highly developed nations, and which for developing nations?

**Answer**: Although developed nations have huge amounts of coastal infrastructure at risk, many developing nations may experience the greatest impacts of global climate change. Because developing countries have less technical expertise and fewer economic resources, they are the ones likely to be least able to respond to the challenges of global climate change. Most developing countries see increased use of fossil fuels as their route to industrial development and resist pressure from highly developed nations to decrease fossil fuel consumption.

---

**Q20.14** [LQ Q13] John Holdren argues there are three possible human responses to climate change: mitigation, adaptation and suffering. Discuss the implications.

**Answer**: Adaptation focuses on learning to live with the environmental changes and societal consequences brought about by global climate change. While some have objected to the development of adaptation strategies because they feel this implies acceptance that global climate change is unavoidable, the widespread observations that the climate is changing make clear that adaptation will be unavoidable. For people today adaptation may mean little or no change but for people 50 years from now agricultural zones will need to be shifted, sea level changes will force people to move and sewage systems to be rebuilt.

**TF20.1** [CW Q9 / AC] Since 1900, the global average temperature has risen by about 10°C (about 15°F).
**Answer: FALSE** — It has risen about 0.8°C, not 10°C.

**TF20.2** [CW Q10 / AC] The albedo of an object refers to the amount of energy it reflects.
**Answer: TRUE**

**TF20.3** [CW Q11 / AC] Milankovitch cycles involve the periodic warming of the Atlantic Ocean due to a reversal of equatorial currents.
**Answer: FALSE** — Milankovitch cycles are Earth's orbital variations: wobble (26,000 years), tilt variation (41,000 years), and orbital elliptical change (100,000 years). This description is El Niño, not Milankovitch.

### SUBJECTIVE / ESSAY

**Q21.1** [AC q1] Write some general effects of Air Pollution. Provide a list.

**Answer**:

2. Vegetation
3. Animals

**Q21.2** [AC q2] What is synergistic effect?

**Answer**: Many air pollutants have synergistic effects — the combined effects are greater than the sum of the separate effects. For example, sulfate and nitrate together do greater damage to the lungs than a combination of the two pollutants would be expected to do based on their separate effects.

---

**Q21.3** [AC q3] Provide list of primary pollutants.

**Answer**:
1. Carbon monoxide (CO) — 58%
2. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) — 11%
3. Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) — 15%
4. Sulfur oxides (SOₓ) — 13%
5. Particulates — 3%
6. Hydrocarbons (H₂S and HF)

---

**Q21.4** [AC q4] Give an example of secondary pollutant.

**Answer**: Ozone (O₃) — formed through photochemical reactions between primary pollutants (NOₓ, VOCs) and sunlight in the lower atmosphere. Also PANs (peroxyacyl nitrates).

---

**Q21.5** [AC q5] How many oxygen molecules are there in ozone?

**Answer**: Ozone (O₃) has three oxygen atoms (not three molecules — one molecule of O₃ contains three oxygen atoms). Normal oxygen (O₂) has two.

---

**Q21.6** [AC q6] What are the sources of hydrogen sulfide?

**Answer**:
1. Natural: geysers
2. Natural: swamps and bogs
3. Human: industrial plants producing petroleum
4. Human: metal smelting

---

**Q21.7** [AC q7] How many are the criteria pollutants?

**Answer**: Six criteria pollutants: (1) Sulfur dioxide, (2) Nitrogen oxides, (3) Carbon monoxide, (4) Ozone, (5) Particulates, (6) Lead. These are the most common pollutants responsible for most air-pollution problems.

---

**Q21.8** [AC q8] What are the main characteristics of Carbon Monoxide (CO)?

**Answer**:
- Colorless, odorless gas
- Even at low concentrations, extremely toxic to humans
- Binds to hemoglobin in blood approximately 250× faster than oxygen
- 90% of CO in atmosphere comes from natural sources
- 10% from fires, automobiles, and incomplete burning of organic compounds
- Effects worse at higher altitudes (where O₂ levels are lower)
- Associated with: heart disease, anemia, respiratory diseases, birth defects, mental retardation

---

**Q21.9** [AC q9] What are the main characteristics of nitrogen oxides?

**Answer**:
- Occur in many forms but largely emitted as NO (nitric oxide) and NO₂ (nitrogen dioxide)
- NO₂ is a yellow-brown to reddish-brown gas
- May be converted to NO₃⁻
- Both subject to emissions regulation and contribute to smog
- Anthropogenic sources: automobiles and power plants (burning fossil fuels)
- Nearly all NO₂ emitted from human sources (combustion of fossil fuels)
- Effects: irritate eyes, nose, throat, mucous membranes; cause viral infections, bronchitis, pneumonia; suppress plant growth
- When converted to nitrate may promote plant growth (eutrophication)
- NO₂ is major contributor to acid rain

---

**Q21.10** [AC q10] What are health impacts of Particulate Matter?

**Answer**:
- 15% to 25% of human mortality in cities associated with PM
- Linked to both lung cancer and bronchitis
- Especially hazardous to elderly and those with asthma
- Heart disease and stroke
- Cell and tissue damage
- Inflammation
- Cholesterol plaque on heart arteries
- PM₂.₅ (<2.5 μm) easily inhaled into lungs, then absorbed into bloodstream
- Ultrafine particles (<0.18 μm) from motor vehicles can enter bloodstream
- Dust can be deposited on plants, interfering with CO₂/O₂ absorption and transpiration
- Block sunlight → global dimming

---

**Q21.11** [AC q11] What is Global dimming?

**Answer**: Global dimming is the gradual reduction in the solar energy that reaches the surface of Earth. It is caused by particulate matter in the atmosphere that blocks sunlight. It cools the atmosphere and lessens global warming.

---

**Q21.12** [AC q12] What are adverse health impacts of Lead?

**Answer**:
- Nervous system damage
- Impaired learning
- Reduced IQ
- Cardiovascular effects
- Kidney damage
- Anemia
- Lead now removed from gas in U.S., Canada, and much of Europe (98% reduction in emissions since 1970s)

---

**Q21.13** [AC q13] What is the list of air toxics?

**Answer**:
1. Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) — rotten egg odor; highly toxic corrosive gas
2. Hydrogen fluoride (HF) — extremely toxic; harms grazing animals
3. Mercury — heavy metal; neurological and developmental damage; biomagnification
4. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) — solvents; hydrocarbons
5. Methyl isocyanate — pesticide ingredient (Sevin); Bhopal disaster (1984) — 2,000 killed, 15,000 injured
6. Benzene (C₆H₆) — gasoline additive, industrial solvent, cigarette smoke
7. Acrolein — volatile hydrocarbon; extremely irritating to nose, eyes, respiratory system
8. Chlorine gases, methanol, ammonia

---

**Q21.14** [AC q14] What are two types of smog?

**Answer**:
1. **Photochemical smog** (LA-type smog or brown air) — directly related to automobile use; formed by reactions of NO₂ and sunlight producing photochemical oxidants (ozone)
2. **Sulfurous smog** (London-type smog, gray air, or industrial smog) — produced by combustion of coal or oil

### REVIEW / SYNTHESIS

**Q21.15** [LQ cross-ref] How does the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990 address air pollution?

**Answer**: Regulations enacted by US Congress addressing: acid rain, toxic emissions, ozone depletion, automobile exhaust, buying and selling of sulfur dioxide emissions. Nitrogen dioxides reduced by 10 million tons. Targets toxins especially those causing cancer.

**Q21.16** [LQ cross-ref] What is the Air Quality Index (AQI) and what do the values mean?

**Answer**: AQI describes air pollution on a given day, determined from measurements of five major pollutants (PM, SO₂, CO, O₃, NO₂). AQI = 100: air is unhealthy; AQI > 200: air pollution alert; AQI > 300: air pollution warning (hazardous to all); AQI > 400: emergency (people requested to remain indoors, minimize exertion).
